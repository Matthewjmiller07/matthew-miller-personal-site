## 🏆 Headliner: The Book of Generations

Every name in the genealogies of the Torah, from Adam to David: twelve lists, 287 names, as a radial family tree and a lifespan timeline you can scrub year by year from Creation. Pick one list (Keturah's sons, Esau's chiefs, the seventy who went down to Egypt, Perez to David) and the tree zooms in.

Then every name is checked against 75 years of Israeli baby names from the Central Bureau of Statistics, which you can filter by year, sex, and Jewish, Muslim, Christian Arab or Druze. Opening a name pulls its Wikipedia summary and the real people on Hebrew Wikipedia who carry it.

What came out:

- **115 of the names have never been given to an Israeli baby.** Cain, Ham, Lot, Terah, Esau and Amalek among them.
- **Jubal is the most-given name in Genesis 4–11:** 40,599 Yuvals.
- **Adam** tied with Amir as the top Druze boys' name in 2000. In 2024 it went to 593 Jewish, 664 Muslim and 42 Druze babies.
- **Iscah,** mentioned once in the whole Torah, hit a record 220 girls in 2024. **Nimrod** peaked at 128 boys in 1983 and fell to 5.
- Count the Arabic forms and **Joseph** (with Yusuf, 108,000), **David** and **Moses** are the most-given names on the list.

It's in the Mega Bible too: on Genesis 4–5 and 10–11 every name is underlined with its lifespan and its Israeli babies. Hover Methuselah: 969 years, 5 babies.

## 🎞️ Kolnoa, and meet Noa

**Kolnoa** is a subtitle language lab. A TED talk plays with its human-made Hebrew and English subtitles in sync; hover a Hebrew word and its English partner lights up, click to save it to your word bank. Five talks so far, easiest first (start with Derek Sivers, 3 minutes). Three ways to play:

- **Fill the gap:** the video pauses, one Hebrew word goes blank, and you pick it from four.
- **Translate:** the video pauses after a line and you translate it, and you're graded word by word on which ideas you caught and whether your Hebrew sounds natural.
- **🔊 Hebrew aloud:** the talks are in English, so this is the only way to hear the Hebrew.

And Kolnoa has a guide now. **Noa** is seven, has curly hair and a flowered dress, and only wants to talk Hebrew. She asks what you like to eat, whether you have a pet, what you want to be when you grow up, and you answer in Hebrew (or English letters if you have no Hebrew keyboard). Every line she says is written in advance and recorded in a real voice; a small judgment model called Jev just decides which reply fits what you said. Get it right and you get a green "נכון!", a burst of hearts and a chime.

She also lives in your browser toolbar: there's a Chrome extension on her page for a two-minute Hebrew chat whenever you need a break.

## ☕ On the bench: a tefillin card and a coffee bar

- **Leshem Yichud tefillin card.** A printable three-colour card of the full Leshem Yichud before tefillin, with nikkud, white on navy in a gold frame. The gold stands taller than the text, so a printer without a colour changer gets all three colours from two filament swaps. Flip it over and there's a mirror for checking your shel rosh is centred, and it has a stand. Three more designs came after: the **Heichal** (arched like the doors of an aron kodesh), a slim **siddur bookmark**, and the **Luchot**. Each prints in about an hour, with files to download.
- **The Coffee Bar.** Fifteen café drinks as cutaway cups you can turn around, each made in front of you one pour at a time: the jug tips, the stream falls, the surface rises, and the latte gets latte art. The point is **kafe hafuch**. The milk goes in first and the espresso is poured through the foam, and because espresso is lighter than hot milk but heavier than foam, it hangs in a tan band in the middle. Also botz, Turkish, ice kafe and nes.

## 🔤 Lost in Translation

A bad translator read קולה as "her voice" instead of cola. You get the bad English and work back to the real word. Sixty-odd cards, from "his dog" (כלבו, department store) to "the father of plaster" (אבטיח, watermelon). Live mode builds new cards from real verses: a machine translator reads single words with no context, and a second model keeps only the genuine misreadings, checked against Sefaria's English.
